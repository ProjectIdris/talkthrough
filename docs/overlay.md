# The overlay renderer

The overlay page (`src/overlay/`) draws the scene language (see `scene-language.md`) over the display it covers and plays scenes sent by the main process. It draws what it is given and nothing else: every scene has already been through the validator, and strings are only ever drawn as text.

## Layers

From back to front inside the overlay window:

1. **Backdrop** (development and frame grabs only): an image shown behind everything. It is also the source of image-card crops.
2. **The stage frame** (optional): a rounded box behind the model, only when the scene asks for `frame`. It never takes the pointer.
3. **The 3D canvas**: one WebGL canvas covering the display. The model floats on it, lit and shadowed, over the page.
4. **The marks canvas**: a 2D canvas covering the display, drawn in device pixels: marks, tags pinned to 3D parts, note cards, image cards, and over them the flat diagrams.
5. **The app's own words**: a listening cue, a thinking indicator, an offer card and a short notice (DOM, from `index.html`).

Scene coordinates run 0 to 1000 across each axis of the window, which covers the display that was captured. Every rectangle is snapped to physical pixels, so lines stay crisp at any display scaling.

## The 3D look

- **Surfaces** (`materials.ts`): physically based (roughness, metalness, a little clear coat) in a named set: plastic, metal, rubber, pcb, glass, glow, paper. A colour tints a surface.
- **Light**: three's room environment, pre-filtered once, plus one key light; Neutral tone mapping so colours keep their identity.
- **Shadow**: a soft shadow on the page under the model (a real shadow map on the higher tiers, and a gradient blob on every tier), on a floor just under the lowest part. The model is never clipped to the stage rectangle: the camera is given the whole display as its window (`setViewOffset`) and the render is scissored to where the model and its shadow are, so cost follows what is drawn, not the display size.
- **Camera**: the longer side of the stage sees 35 degrees, so a wide stage does not skew the edges. Zoom 1 frames the model: the distance comes from a sphere round the visible parts (their own bounding spheres, so a part turning on its axis does not make the camera breathe) with a margin of 10 per cent (a tube thick for its length, such as a stem or a vessel, counts as part of the model; a thin cable does not), looking at the middle of that sphere unless a part is named; an opened (exploded) model is already part of the sphere, so the camera stands back for it. The framing sphere eases to the model's (about half a second to settle) when parts are added or the model opens, so the model does not leap smaller at each step; a frame grab or a test without a clock takes it as it is. The render's scissor has a margin of a few per cent round the model and its shadow, so a lit edge or a part just past its box is never cut.
- **Edges**: thin dark lines on solids, from the second tier up.
- **Tiers** (`TIERS` in `materials.ts`): `low` (no multisampling, no shadow map, no edge lines, no clear coat), `mid` (the default on software rendering: multisampling, a 1024 shadow map, edges, clear coat), `high` (2048 shadow map, device pixel ratio up to 2, and a finishing pass in `post.ts`: the scene is drawn into a multisampled target that keeps its depth, ambient occlusion (GTAO) darkens creases and contacts, then tone mapping and SMAA bring it to the canvas, which stays transparent; the targets are the size of the model's rectangle (rounded up), not of the window, and a model whose rectangle is over about 4 million device pixels, a graphics stack without half-float render targets, or a failed first draw gets the plain render; see-through parts are blended in linear light there, so they look a little lighter than on the lower tiers). The page address takes `?tier=low|mid|high`; with none it picks `mid` on a software rasteriser and `high` otherwise.

## Building models

`geometry.ts` makes the shapes (rounded boxes, extrusions, lathes, tubes along a curve with an optional radius profile, drawn on a ring at a time); `blob.ts` makes the soft organic forms: lumps add up to a field, the skin is cut out of the field by marching tetrahedra with the normals taken from the field (smooth shading, an exact ellipsoid for a lone lump), over a grid of cells chosen from the tier and the shape's thinnest side, capped at 160,000 triangles a blob and, over a whole scene, at a budget by tier (40,000, 90,000 or 180,000): a blob built after the budget is used is cut with fewer cells (never dropped, never below 2,500 triangles), so ten blobs cannot build 100,000 triangles on the page thread; `parts.ts` builds the parts kit from the same pieces, fitted to the box a scene gives; `scene3d.ts` brings a Frame into three.js, applies explode (children sit at their position times one plus the spread, or at position plus their own `explode` offset times the spread), places the floor, light and shadow, and projects points of objects to the screen for pins and hit tests.

## Looks

`theme.ts` holds the one look of tags, cards and image cards: dark glass, a coloured dot or bar for each item, thin light lines, Windows' own fonts (Segoe UI Variable). The scene never chooses it.

### Where tags go

`labels.ts` places the tag of every `pin` by itself; the scene only says what is pinned to what. It is plain geometry (tested without a window): the page gives it the outline of every part of the model on screen, how busy the page underneath is (edges in the screenshot, so text counts), and the anchor of each pin. A tag is put where it overlaps neither the model, nor another tag, nor (as far as there is room) the page's own text; its leader never crosses another leader or tag, and runs over as little of the model as possible. Placed tags stay put while the model turns; only the leaders follow their parts. When the model moves a long way (a new stage, a big zoom) the tags are laid out again and glide to their new places; a tag whose way would take it through another (two that swap sides) fades out where it is and in at its new place instead, so tags never pass over each other. A scene's `off` is only a hint for the side.

## Time

`timeline.ts` is the clock-free core. A step becomes a plan (`planStep`) that starts from the state the previous step settled in, and a plan plus a time becomes a complete frame to draw (`evaluate`). Because later steps start from the settled state, objects, ids and the camera carry across steps and across follow-up questions. Actions are applied in time order (`at`, then list order). Paths (`along`) are splines, and `reveal` and `explode` are ordinary tweens.

In real time (`player.ts`):

- Each step is spoken through the `Voice` adapter, and the step's drawing starts when the voice's `start` event fires.
- The step's clock is scaled to the voice's own estimate of how long it will speak, so the actions run over the utterance.
- The next step is spoken once its speech has ended and the step's drawing has finished.
- A voice that never starts does not stall the picture: after 2.5 s, or as soon as it reports the end, the drawing plays anyway.
- Steps may arrive while earlier ones play (`begin`, `step`, `end` messages): they queue. Running out of steps is not the end of the turn until `end` has come.
- A step can also arrive piece by piece, as the model writes it: `step-open` brings the words (spoken at once; the step's clock starts with them), each `action` joins the open step and is drawn when it arrives with its own build-in motion (a model assembles in view as its parts come), and `step-close` says the step is whole. An action keeps the order it arrives in, and starts at its authored `at` or at the moment it arrives, whichever is later. The step stays the unit of timing: it does not end, and the next one does not begin, until it is closed, spoken and drawn.
- `hush` stops the speech at once and drops queued steps and the open step's later actions; the step in progress finishes drawing.
- After the last step a model that is on screen turns slowly by itself (about 5 degrees a second after a pause, easing in) until the user takes hold of it or the scene fades; a follow-up carries on from where it got to.
- A new turn continues the picture on screen (a follow-up); the first turn of a question clears it first.

## Fading

When a turn has finished, the scene stays at full strength for `FADE_AFTER_MS` (set in `src/shared/ipc.ts`), then fades over 1.2 s, unless the pointer is on the model or the app's words; touching it starts the count again. A faded scene takes no clicks. It is hidden, not cleared, so a follow-up still reaches its ids; the next question or Clear removes it. `fade.ts` is the logic and is tested without a clock.

## Turning, zooming and opening the finished model

Once a model's steps are done (nothing is playing or being written), the person can handle it. The pointer region is the model's screen bounds plus a 28 px margin (`orbit.ts`), so a thin part is easy to catch and the page around it stays clickable; the page tells the main process `setInteractive(true)` only there, and click-through comes back the moment the pointer leaves (the drag's end is in a `finally`, so a thrown error cannot leave the window solid).

- **Drag** turns the camera (0.4 degrees a pixel). The drag is folded into the camera's tweens, so a camera action after it starts from where the camera is. The slow idle turn stops while it is held.
- **Wheel** zooms (`wheelFactor`: a gentle step, clamped, mouse wheels and touchpads alike), within the scene language's own zoom limits (0.2 to 5), so the first wheel step never moves a scene's own zoom.
- **Double click** puts the camera back where the scene left it and closes the model.
- **Explode / Assemble** (a small dark button above the model's top-right corner, with Cut under it, and More detail on its left; the group sits clear of the model's bounds, beside or below it when there is no room above, and tags keep off it) and the **E** key while the pointer is over the model open the parts outward and close them again, easing over 0.9 s either way. A part with its own `explode` offset moves along it; the rest move away from the middle of the parts around them, further the deeper they are (`explode.ts`, tested without a window); the distance grows ever more slowly past 6 units so a tower under its head does not leave the picture. The camera stands back by how far the opened model reaches beyond the closed one. Tags and their leaders follow the parts and keep the no-crossing layout.
- **E** is a system-wide key that the app holds only while it is wanted: after the person has pressed on the model (or its button), while the pointer is still on it, the click lease is held and the pen is off (`keyWanted`, `createKeyHold` in `interaction.ts`). It is given back the moment the pointer leaves, the lease runs out, the pen goes on or the window closes, and when the app exits. The overlay window never takes focus, so the program the person is using keeps its keyboard throughout; the page gets a `toggle-explode` message and acts on it only while the pointer is on a settled model.

The main process also keeps a lease on "solid": the page repeats `setInteractive(true)` every 3 s while the pointer is over the model, and if that stops (page hung, pointer lost without a word) the window goes back to click-through after 8 s (`createClickGuard`, tested with fake timers). The page's side of it is `createHold` (`orbit.ts`): it works out "solid" from where the pointer is and where the model is, and does so again on every beat, so a model that fades or is cleared under a resting pointer lets go at once (or at the next beat for a button), and a beat never renews a claim that no longer holds. While the pen is on the window takes the mouse whatever the page says, as before.

Buttons: the window learns the pointer is over a button only from a mouse move, so a tap, or a click with no move before it, would go to the app beneath. The page therefore reports where its buttons are (the escalation offer, Explode, Cut, More detail; `reportButtons` in `overlay.ts`), and main lays a small window of its own, never click-through, exactly over each (`src/main/button-catchers.ts`, `button-window.ts`); a press on one comes back as a `press` message and the page clicks that button. They are put away while the pen is on, and the overlay stays click-through everywhere else. The Yes and No the app asks before acting for the person (guide navigation) is a window of its own in the same way (`consent-card.ts`).

- **More detail** (a second small button to the left of Explode, once per answer, only when a detail model is set up) asks main for a detail pass. The scene messages for it are an ordinary turn with `begin` carrying `detail: true` and a `status` of `detail` while it runs: the page then clears nothing (whatever the turn number), speaks nothing (the words only name what each step adds), keeps the offer and notices as they are, and an object whose id is already on screen is replaced in place by the one that arrives. The answer is checked against a state that knows the ids already on screen: it may name one it does not write again (a pin on it, a part under it) and may write any of them once more, richer. The person's explode and the camera a double click returns to are left alone. See the setup notes for the settings.

Frame grabs can show these states without a pointer: `--steps step:t:az:el:zoom:explode` (leave a field empty to keep the scene's value), for example `--steps 7:20:55:22:1.2:1`.

## Where things go

A model writes coordinates without seeing the page, so the overlay moves what it would cover (`src/overlay/placement.ts`). From a small gray picture of the screenshot it finds where there is something on the page (an edge, or a tone that is not the page's own background) and, for the 3D stage (shrunk to three quarters of its size at most, so it stays big enough to read; a tag beside a small stage is drawn up to 30 per cent smaller so it does not outweigh the model) and for each note card or crop, picks the nearest free rectangle: off the text, off what the answer points at (boxes, circles, underlines, arrow ends, labels, crop regions), off the stage and off the cards still showing. The stage may come out smaller (down to about half) to fit, and a stage the model asked for small (under about a quarter of the screen across) is made larger, up to twice its size, where the blank page beside it has room (`enlarge`); touching what the answer points at, even by a corner, costs it a good deal, so it goes smaller before it covers the figure the answer boxed. What is covered is weighed by kind (`bodyTextOf`, `FIXTURE_COST`): each cell of ink is body text to some degree from 0 to 1, and covering the rest counts six times as much. A stack of at least four lines that start together, repeat at a steady pitch and mostly run nearly as long as the longest is a column of text however narrow it is (a split pane, a chat, an ultrawide screen); any other ink is weighed by the width of its line, a fixture under 15% of the page, prose from 30%, and a ramp between so a figure does not flip at a cliff, and a run wider than 85% is a bar (a toolbar) and a fixture again. A sidebar or panel of labels of all lengths, an infobox and its picture, and a toolbar are fixtures, so a flat diagram goes smaller before it covers the page's controls or figures (down to 0.64 of its size, as narrow as half its width, never under 150 units a side), and covers body text last. A place that is already free stays where the model put it, a move has to be clearly worth it, and a page with nowhere free is left as written. A card's place is free again when it is cleared. The scene's messages wait, up to a second and a half, for the screenshot to be decoded so the first stage is placed on the real page; without a picture of the page nothing moves. The optional `frame` behind the stage is light over a light page and dark over a dark one.

A model also points at things inside a picture from a downsized view, so a mark can be a few pixels off what it means (an arrow stopping short of its curve, a ring beside the crossing, a box with an edge through a line of text). `src/overlay/snap.ts` looks at the page's own pixels, at about a thousand cells across: the long edges (lines and curves, found by thinning the edges and keeping those that run a few percent of the screen; letters are shorter), the corners and crossings (where the gradient changes two ways, then placed exactly where the two lines through them meet) and the ink (anything that is not the page's own background). Then an arrow's tip goes to the nearest line, a point goes to the nearest crossing or line, a small ring is centred on the crossing next to it, a polygon's corners go to the page's corners, a trace is laid on the curve it follows, a box takes in an edge that cuts through a line of text or a figure and takes in a loose empty margin (keeping a little air), and an underline drops to just under its line of letters. It never moves a mark far (seven percent of the shorter side at most, and less for most) and it leaves a mark alone wherever it cannot tell what was meant: nothing long enough to land on near it, two lines about equally near, a mark over blank page, a box on a border, a tilted box, a large ring round a figure. It runs before the placement above, once for each action, and says each move in the app log (`talkthrough: snapped arrow demandarrow by 14 units`). What it works out from a page (the busy map, the placement grid, the snapper) is kept for that capture only and dropped when the next screenshot arrives, since one image element holds every capture; it is built as soon as the screenshot has decoded, not at the first mark.

**Real controls.** On Windows a window can say where its own buttons, tabs, menu items and fields are, so a mark aimed at one need not be guessed from pixels. When the chord is complete (not on a lone Right Ctrl) and the person has turned `controlSnap` on (it is off by default), the app asks one PowerShell helper, started the first time it is asked and kept for the session (`src/main/ps-helper.ts`; nothing is compiled or written for a request, since user32 is called through an in-memory assembly, and the request takes about 20 ms after the first, which takes about a second), to read the window in front through the .NET UI Automation that ships with Windows PowerShell (`src/main/uia.ts`, `uia-script.ts`: nothing to install, read-only by construction: it asks only for each control's rectangle, kind and whether it is off screen, and never invokes, selects, focuses or types into anything, and never reads a name or a value). **A browser window is never read** (Chromium, Electron, Firefox, Qt, found by the window classes before the accessibility API is touched): asking a browser to build its accessibility tree turns accessibility on in the whole browser until it is restarted, which costs it CPU, so marks there snap to the picture alone. The rectangles become boxes on the 0..1000 scale and are sent as a `controls` message after the screenshot. The answer never waits for it: the model takes seconds, and a read that is late, fails, finds nothing or is cut off at 2.5 seconds leaves marks snapping to the picture as above. `src/shared/controls.ts` then puts a box on the control it overlaps best (a clear match of about the same size, with no second control about as good: a box round a whole form, over blank page or between two buttons is left alone) a little outside its bounds, a ring round it, a point inside a small control at its middle, and an arrow that stops just short of a control onto its edge; a mark put on a control is not also snapped to the picture, and each move is said in the log (`talkthrough: control-snapped box b1 by 18.8 units (button)`). The next screenshot, and the guide's fresh look after a click, drop the old controls and read the new screen. Other systems have no reader. `--controls <boxes.json>` on `scripts/overlay-frames.cjs` plays a scene with a given list, for frames. The helper is read-only as a whole unless `guideNavigation` is also on: only then is the script that can select a tab, expand a submenu or scroll (`nav-script.ts`, native windows only, behind the guard in `src/shared/nav-guard.ts`) added to it; see "Guide steps" in `docs/scene-language.md`.

Marks that are strokes (box, circle, underline, arrow, polygon, trace) are drawn on like a pen: 0.8 to 1.2 seconds by length, the leading end feathered, and wiped off along the line when cleared. With the system's "reduce motion" setting they appear and go in one step.

## Flat diagrams

`diagram-plan.ts` plans them in the timeline (a diagram and its pieces have a birth, a death, and changes of colour, opacity, tick and pulse; a piece written again under its id takes the old one's place and key), `diagram-layout.ts` decides where the pieces go inside the rectangle as pure functions of the pieces and the size (layers for a flow, which a long chain of single nodes wraps into two or three rows, each running back the way the last came, when that makes the boxes clearly larger, and which leaves room for a loop back and its label to swing out inside the rectangle; an axis with non-overlapping labels for a timeline, rows for steps, nice ticks and a map from chart units for a chart), and `diagram-draw.ts` draws them in the look of the cards: dark glass, a coloured accent, thin light lines. A piece that is not there yet still counts toward the layout, so a diagram that builds as the model writes it does not jump about; a later arrival moves the others and they glide (the page keeps asking for frames until they are in place). Edges and series are drawn along from their start as the voice reaches them. The diagram's rectangle is placed like a card, and may be shrunk a little to fit a free place, because the drawing scales to it. Failing a blank place the size asked for, it takes one that is taller and narrower (down to about half the width, the same area) before it covers text, so it can sit in a margin or beside a column; a list of steps or a chart asked for more than 440 or 520 units of width is placed at that. A chart is never squeezed into a strip: it keeps at least 1.1 times as wide as tall on the screen's pixels (`DIAGRAM_MIN_RATIO`), and gives up size (down to 0.64) before shape. A node's note stays in its box however short the box is (the text and the note go small, down to 9 pixels), and a column of nodes with notes gives its boxes more of their cell. A mark written after the diagram and inside the rectangle it was asked for (a ring on its equilibrium) belongs to it (one written before it points at the page and stays put, as it does when the answer is drawn as it arrives): it is not snapped to the page or kept clear of, and goes with the diagram to where it is placed, to the same spot in the new rectangle (`followDiagram` in `placement.ts`), and is drawn over the diagram's card, not under it. Only the marks the page takes as the diagram's own are (`ownedMarkIds`, `splitOwned`); a mark pointing at the page that a diagram ends up covering stays under the card. A card or diagram that was not asked for in the top fifth of the screen keeps out of it unless the rest of the page is a good deal worse, because an app's own bar, tabs and menus are thin and look nearly blank (`TOP_BAND` in `placement.ts`).

## Cutting a model open

`cut` (and the Cut button beside Explode, which cuts the whole model through its middle from the front and puts it back) removes everything beyond a plane and caps the cut faces (`src/overlay/cutaway.ts`). The plane is a three.js clipping plane on the surfaces of the cut object and everything in it. A cap is the stencil trick, once for each closed part: its back faces add one to the stencil, its front faces take one away, and a cap is drawn on the plane where what is left is not zero, in a paler tone of the part's colour. A part that is an open surface (marked `open` in the kit builders, as the bore liner is) has no cap, since the count means nothing for it. Caps hang from the scene, not from the model, so they do not count toward its size, the framing or its outline. The renderer is made with a stencil buffer and local clipping on.

## The pen

While the keys are held the main process sends `pen on` and the window takes the mouse. The page (`pen.ts`, plain state tested without a window) draws what is dragged as a soft marker: a translucent ribbon in the style's red with a faint wider halo, no dark outline, thickest at the pen and tapering at its start and tip, about 0.7 percent of the longer side wide. The line is set at an even spacing and smoothed, so a shaky hand reads as a calm loop. It reports the stroke with `stroke`: points on the 0 to 1000 scale, appended per stroke id, once per frame while drawing and once more when the button comes up (`done`). A click, or a twitch under 3 units, draws and reports nothing. While the pen is on the mouse never turns the model or clicks the app's own words, and the browser's own image drag is switched off (it would cancel the stroke).

**Decay.** What is drawn settles, like a laser pointer's tail: over about a second behind the pen each point loses opacity and thickness until it is a muted trace at about a fifth of full strength, and the drawing stays that muted until the answer starts. The decay is only how it looks: every point is kept, and the main process gets the whole stroke (the crop and the picture the model sees are made from all of it). With reduced motion there is no decay: the strokes stay at full strength. After `pen off` the strokes stay until the answer's first step (or the end, a notice, a clear or an idle status), then fade over 0.6 s; a new `pen on` removes them at once. It is never part of a scene, and the screenshot kept for image cards is the clean one.

**Undo.** While the keys are held and a circle is drawn, a small *Undo* button sits beside the last circle (it hides when nothing is left to take back, and while a circle is being drawn). A click on it takes back the last circle: the page sends `{ id, points: [], done: true, removed: true }`, and main drops that stroke from the picture, the crop and the numbering. A press on the button never starts a stroke. There is no key for it: with the chord's modifiers held, a Z would reach the app underneath as a redo.

**Cancel.** A right click while the keys are held cancels the whole question: the drawing goes at once, no screenshot or recording is sent, and a short *Cancelled.* notice shows. The window keeps the mouse until the right button is up again, so neither the press nor the release reaches the app underneath.

## Looking at it without the app

`scripts/overlay-frames.cjs` renders a scene with the overlay page in a hidden, software-rendered Electron window and saves PNG frames, either at chosen moments (`--steps 2:1.5,5:3`) or by playing the scene in real time (`--play`):

```
npm run build
npx electron scripts/overlay-frames.cjs samples/scenes/pin-tumbler-lock.json --out frames \
    --steps 0:3,5:3 --backdrop samples/pages/lock-article.svg --tier mid
```

Offscreen windows always render at a device scale of 1; add `--hidden --dpr 2` to render in a hidden window at another scale (the PNG is then at physical size).

`samples/pages/` has backdrops drawn to the layouts the samples' marks were written against. A follow-up is rendered after its first scene with `--after samples/scenes/earth-layers.json`.

## Software rendering

The renderer works without a GPU: Electron with hardware acceleration off runs WebGL on the system's software rasteriser. The mid tier is meant for that; the high tier is judged on a real GPU: `--gpu yes` on the frame tool leaves hardware acceleration on, `--clean yes` leaves the app's own buttons out of the picture, and `--metrics yes` prints what the graphics process holds and times twenty seeks.
